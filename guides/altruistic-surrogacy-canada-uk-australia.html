In altruistic systems a surrogate helps another family without being paid beyond her expenses. Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia are the best-known examples. The model protects against commercial pressure, but it also means long waits, careful record-keeping and legal steps after the birth. Here's how each country works in practice.

Canada

The law. The federal Assisted Human Reproduction Act (2004) allows surrogacy but prohibits paying a surrogate, offering or advertising payment, and paying or accepting payment for arranging a surrogate's services. Penalties reach C$500,000 and/or ten years in prison. The surrogate must be at least 21.

Reimbursement. Since 2020, federal regulations set out which expenditures can be reimbursed and require receipts. Typical categories include travel, medications and medical devices, maternity clothing, counselling, legal advice, insurance premiums and, with a medical certificate, lost work income. Keep every receipt and use a trust account or escrow.

Matching. Because brokers can't be paid to match, surrogates are often friends, relatives or people met through community groups. Consultancies offer paid support services such as coordination and education.

Parentage. Provincial law decides how intended parents become legal parents, often through a court declaration or an administrative process after the surrogate's consent. Quebec introduced a framework in 2023 that requires a notarised agreement before pregnancy.

Who can access it. Singles, same-sex couples and international intended parents can all pursue surrogacy in Canada, subject to provincial rules.

United Kingdom

The law. The Surrogacy Arrangements Act 1985 bans commercial surrogacy agencies and advertising; the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008 governs parenthood. Surrogacy agreements are not enforceable.

Expenses. Surrogates may receive reasonable expenses; the HFEA notes typical payments of around GBP 10,000-15,000. The family court reviews payments before making a parental order and must authorise anything beyond reasonable expenses.

Matching. Non-profit organisations such as Surrogacy UK, Brilliant Beginnings, COTS and My Surrogacy Journey help people meet, usually building a relationship before conception. Clinics may not find a surrogate for you.

Parentage. The surrogate is the legal mother at birth. Intended parents apply for a parental order after the birth; guidance has long advised doing so within six months. At least one intended parent must be genetically related, and single people have been able to apply since 2019.

Reform. In 2023 the Law Commissions of England and Wales and of Scotland recommended a new pathway recognising intended parents from birth after pre-conception safeguards. The government has said reform cannot be prioritised for now, and in 2026 it again declined to bring it forward after a petition debate.

Australia

The law. Every state and territory allows altruistic surrogacy (the Northern Territory since 2022), and commercial surrogacy is a criminal offence. Residents of New South Wales, Queensland and the ACT also commit an offence by arranging commercial surrogacy overseas.

Requirements. States generally require counselling, independent legal advice for both sides and, in some states, approval before treatment. Rules about who can access surrogacy differ slightly by state.

Expenses. Reasonable expenses such as medical, legal, counselling and travel costs, and some lost earnings, may be reimbursed.

Parentage. Intended parents apply to a state court for a parentage order after the birth.

What's next. In 2026 the Australian Law Reform Commission has been reviewing surrogacy laws, including whether surrogates should be compensated. Australian data also show many more children born to Australian parents through overseas surrogacy than at home.

Making an altruistic journey work

  • Be patient and visible: join reputable non-profit communities and events.
  • Build the relationship first: in all three countries, the relationship carries the journey because contracts are weaker or unenforceable.
  • Document everything: expenses, consent, counselling and legal advice.
  • Get early legal advice about parentage in the province or state of birth.
  • Never pay "under the table". It can be a criminal offence and can jeopardise the parentage process.
