Two legal questions sit at the centre of every surrogacy journey. What have we agreed? That is the gestational carrier agreement. And who are the child's legal parents? That is parentage. Getting both right protects the surrogate, the intended parents and, most of all, the child.

This guide is general information. Surrogacy law is local and technical, so every party needs a lawyer licensed in the right place.

Why each side needs its own lawyer

The surrogate and the intended parents have different interests: one is carrying a pregnancy and taking on medical risk; the other is paying and will become the parents. A single lawyer can't fairly represent both. Standard practice, and often a legal or agency requirement, is:

  • the intended parents hire a reproductive lawyer licensed where the surrogate lives;
  • the surrogate chooses her own independent lawyer, and the intended parents pay that lawyer's fee;
  • no medications start until the agreement is signed by everyone.

What a gestational carrier agreement covers

A well-drafted agreement usually includes:

  1. The parties and intentions: who the intended parents are, that the surrogate does not intend to parent, and that the embryo is not genetically hers.
  2. Medical process: the clinic, the number of embryos per transfer, the maximum number of transfers, and prenatal testing.
  3. Decisions in difficult scenarios: discussions about reduction or termination, while recognising the surrogate's right to make decisions about her own body.
  4. Payments: compensation where lawful, or permitted expenses, how and when they are paid through escrow, and what happens if the journey ends early.
  5. Insurance: health coverage, life and disability insurance, and who pays deductibles and the newborn's costs.
  6. Conduct and health: lifestyle commitments such as no smoking, travel limits late in pregnancy, and attending appointments.
  7. Communication and confidentiality: updates, social media and privacy.
  8. Birth plan and parentage: the court process, hospital arrangements and paperwork.
  9. Contingencies: the death or incapacity of intended parents (often with guardianship arrangements), divorce, disputes and governing law.

In jurisdictions where agreements are unenforceable (such as the UK), a written agreement still matters: it records everyone's understanding and helps the court later.

How intended parents become legal parents

Pre-birth orders (supportive US states)

In supportive states, a court issues a pre-birth order during the second or third trimester. It declares the intended parents the legal parents and directs the hospital and vital records office to name them on the original birth certificate. Some states make this available to all family types; others limit it by marital status or genetic link. See the US state guide.

Post-birth orders and validations

Some states issue post-birth orders. Texas and Utah use court-validated agreements before pregnancy; in Texas, validation requires the intended parents to be married. A few states may require a second-parent or step-parent adoption for a non-genetic parent.

UK parental orders

In the UK the surrogate is the legal mother at birth, and if she is married or in a civil partnership her spouse may be the second legal parent. Intended parents apply to the family court for a parental order after the birth. Conditions include that at least one intended parent is genetically related to the child, that the surrogate consents freely (not earlier than six weeks after birth), that only reasonable expenses were paid unless the court authorises more, and that the child lives with the applicants. Guidance has long advised applying within six months of the birth. Since 2019 single people can apply.

Canada

Parentage is provincial. Several provinces allow intended parents to be declared parents by a court or through an administrative process after the surrogate's consent; Quebec introduced its own framework in 2023 requiring a notarised agreement before pregnancy. Ask a lawyer in the province where the baby will be born.

Other countries

Some countries name intended parents on the birth certificate at birth (for example, Ukraine for eligible married couples); others require court proceedings, DNA evidence or adoption. International families must also make sure their home country recognises the parentage. See our guide to international surrogacy.

Common legal pitfalls

  • Crossing state lines: the surrogate lives in one state but delivers in another with different rules.
  • Marital status: a married surrogate's spouse may be presumed a parent unless the order addresses it.
  • Unmarried or single intended parents in states with marriage requirements.
  • Starting medication before signatures.
  • Paying outside the agreement, which can breach law in expenses-only systems.
  • Forgetting the home country in international journeys.
