Surrogacy is one of the main ways gay men, single men and some transgender and non-binary people become parents to a genetically related child. It's also used by single women who cannot carry. The medical steps are the same as for anyone else; the differences are mainly legal and practical.

Where surrogacy is open to you

Access depends on the law where the surrogate lives and gives birth, and on your home country's recognition rules.

  • Generally open to LGBTQ+ couples and single parents: most US states, Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and, for residents, Israel (since 2022).
  • Restricted: Georgia and Ukraine accept married heterosexual couples only; Greece excluded single men and male couples in 2025; India allows only married heterosexual Indian couples and some widowed or divorced women.
  • Uncertain: in Mexico and Colombia access is shaped by courts and state rules, and can change.

Use the law tracker and compare countries to check your options.

Building embryos: donors and decisions

Male couples and single men need an egg donor and a gestational carrier, who should be different people. Decisions to make early:

  • Known or identity-release donor? Many families choose donors open to future contact, so the child can learn about their origins.
  • Whose sperm? Couples often fertilise eggs from the same donor with both partners' sperm, then decide which embryo to transfer first. Some plan a second journey with the other partner's embryos so siblings share the donor's genetics.
  • How many embryos? Most clinics recommend single-embryo transfers; some families plan ahead for siblings.

Single women and couples where one partner has eggs but cannot carry may use their own eggs with a carrier.

Making both parents legal parents

For couples, the goal is for both parents to be legal parents from the start, not just the genetic one.

  • In supportive US states, pre-birth orders can name both parents regardless of genetics or marital status. Some states still require marriage or a second-parent adoption; check the US state guide.
  • In the UK, couples (married, in a civil partnership or living as partners) and, since 2019, single people can apply for a parental order; at least one applicant must be genetically related.
  • In Canada, provincial rules generally recognise both intended parents.
  • Back home, make sure your country recognises both parents. Some recognise only the genetic father and require the other parent to adopt.

Finding inclusive professionals

Ask agencies and clinics how many LGBTQ+ and single-parent journeys they completed last year, whether their surrogate pool is comfortable with your family, and whether their paperwork and staff use inclusive language. Non-profits such as Men Having Babies and Family Equality publish resources, and some offer financial-assistance programmes.

Costs

Costs are similar to any journey, plus an egg donor if needed, which often adds US$25,000-50,000 in the US. Use the cost calculator and choose "Need donor eggs".

Talking to your child

Children of LGBTQ+ and single parents through surrogacy benefit, like all donor-conceived and surrogacy-born children, from hearing their story early and often, in simple words. See our guide to emotional health and telling your child.
