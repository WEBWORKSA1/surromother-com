A surrogacy journey is a long project with many parties. The good news is that the order of events is fairly predictable. Below are the ten stages most intended parents go through, what happens at each, and how long it usually takes. Timings are typical for a US agency journey; we note where altruistic or international routes differ.

Stage 1: Get medical and legal advice (2-6 weeks)

Start with a reproductive endocrinologist (fertility specialist). They will confirm whether a gestational carrier is the right option, review any previous IVF, and advise on creating embryos. Then speak to a reproductive lawyer about the law where you live: whether surrogacy is lawful, whether you can pay a surrogate, how parenthood will be recognised, and, if you live abroad, how your child would get citizenship.

Do this before paying any agency. The answers shape every later choice.

Stage 2: Create or secure embryos (2-4 months, can overlap)

Most clinics and many agencies want frozen, genetically tested embryos before matching. That means an IVF cycle with the intended mother's eggs or with donor eggs, fertilisation with the intended father's or donor sperm, and often preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-A) to screen for chromosomal problems.

If you need an egg donor, add time to choose one and synchronise cycles. Male couples often create embryos with both partners' sperm and transfer one at a time.

Stage 3: Choose your route and team (2-6 weeks)

You will decide between:

  • An agency or programme, which recruits and screens surrogates, matches you and coordinates the journey for a fee.
  • An independent journey, usually with a surrogate you already know, where you engage the clinic, lawyers, counsellor and escrow company yourself.
  • A non-profit organisation in altruistic systems such as the UK, which supports matching through community rather than as a business.

Interview at least two or three providers. Our 30-question agency checklist covers licensing, screening, escrow, fees and what happens if an agency fails.

Stage 4: Matching (1-6 months in the US; often 12+ months in altruistic systems)

You and potential surrogates review each other's profiles and talk, usually by video. A good match aligns on:

  • how often and how you will communicate during and after the pregnancy;
  • views on prenatal testing, selective reduction and termination;
  • the number of embryos to transfer (most clinics now recommend one);
  • who will be present at appointments and the birth;
  • logistics such as travel distance and the surrogate's work and family.

Both sides must agree. Take your time: the relationship carries you through the next year or more.

Stage 5: Screening (4-8 weeks)

The surrogate has a medical evaluation at your IVF clinic, including blood tests, infectious-disease screening and an assessment of her uterus. She, and usually her partner, complete a psychological evaluation with a licensed mental-health professional. Agencies also run background checks. Intended parents may have their own counselling session and infectious-disease testing required by regulators.

Some agencies screen surrogates before matching, which reduces the chance of a match falling through at this stage.

Stage 6: The legal agreement (3-6 weeks)

The surrogate and the intended parents each have their own lawyer. The intended parents pay for both. The gestational carrier agreement covers compensation or permitted expenses, insurance, medical decisions, confidentiality, risks, what happens in difficult scenarios and how parentage will be established. Medication should not start until it is signed.

In altruistic systems agreements may be unenforceable (as in the UK), but they still record everyone's intentions and should be written with legal advice. Read more in our guide to contracts and parentage.

Stage 7: Medication and embryo transfer (4-6 weeks)

The surrogate takes medications, typically estrogen and then progesterone, to prepare her uterine lining, with blood tests and ultrasounds to check progress. When the lining is ready, the clinic thaws an embryo and transfers it through a thin catheter, a short procedure that usually needs no anaesthetic.

A pregnancy blood test follows about nine to ten days after a day-5 transfer. Our due date calculator shows every milestone from the transfer date.

Stage 8: Pregnancy (about 9 months)

The fertility clinic monitors early pregnancy, usually until 8-10 weeks, then releases the surrogate to her own obstetrician. Agree in advance how you'll hear news from appointments, how you'll handle the anatomy scan and any complications, and how much contact everyone wants. Compensation or reimbursements are paid through escrow according to the agreement.

Stage 9: Birth (a few days)

Make a hospital plan before the third trimester: who is in the delivery room, who holds the baby first, where the baby stays, how feeding will work, and what paperwork the hospital needs. Confirm the hospital knows about the parentage order or legal process.

Stage 10: Parentage, birth certificate and going home (days to months)

In supportive US states a pre-birth order lets the intended parents be named on the original birth certificate. Elsewhere there may be a post-birth court order, an adoption, or, in the UK, a parental order after the birth. International parents also need the child's citizenship, passport and permission to travel, which can take weeks.

Planning tips that save time and money

  • Embryos first. Tested embryos make the journey faster and outcomes easier to predict.
  • Budget for a second transfer and a 10% contingency. See the cost calculator.
  • Check insurance early. A surrogate whose plan excludes surrogacy needs a separate policy, which can cost tens of thousands of dollars.
  • Keep everything in writing, including fee schedules and refund terms.
  • Think about the surrogate's experience. Respect, clear communication and prompt payments are what make journeys go well.
