Surrogacy is one of the most expensive ways to build a family, and published prices vary wildly because they include different things. This guide breaks the cost into its parts so you can compare quotes like for like. All figures are indicative ranges in US dollars, compiled from prices that agencies, clinics and non-profits published in 2026.

The headline numbers

Destination Typical total for intended parents Why it differs
United States US$100,000-250,000+ Surrogate compensation, agency fees, insurance and legal work
Canada About US$45,000-85,000 Expenses-only model; no agency matching fees allowed
United Kingdom About US$32,000-85,000 Reasonable expenses only (often GBP 10,000-15,000) plus IVF
Mexico About US$60,000-100,000 Lower medical costs; court steps for parentage
Colombia About US$55,000-90,000 Lower costs; no statute and a ban bill pending
Georgia About US$50,000-75,000 Married heterosexual couples only; long waits

Published US agency estimates range from about US$100,000-200,000 at large national agencies to US$178,000-282,000 at one premium agency for US intended parents. The spread comes mostly from what is included and where the surrogate lives.

The cost, line by line (United States)

1. Agency or programme fee: US$20,000-45,000. Recruitment, screening and matching of surrogates, coordination, and support. Some agencies offer fixed-price or "protection" programmes that bundle extra transfers or rematch costs at a higher price.

2. Surrogate base compensation: about US$50,000-80,000 for a first-time surrogate, more for experienced surrogates and in premium states such as California. It is usually paid in monthly instalments once the pregnancy is confirmed. See our guide to how compensation works.

3. Surrogate allowances and reimbursements: US$8,000-22,000. Monthly allowance, maternity clothes, travel, lost wages, childcare and housekeeping during bed rest, and fees tied to medical events such as transfers, invasive procedures or a caesarean.

4. IVF and embryos: US$0 if you already have embryos; roughly US$15,000-30,000 per IVF cycle with your own eggs; US$35,000-60,000 with donor eggs (including donor compensation and agency fees). Genetic testing adds cost per embryo.

5. Surrogate screening, transfer and monitoring: US$7,000-15,000. Clinic evaluation, medications, the transfer itself and early-pregnancy monitoring. A second transfer repeats much of this.

6. Legal: US$10,000-25,000. Separate lawyers for each side, agreement drafting and negotiation, the parentage order, and birth-certificate work. Complex or international cases cost more.

7. Insurance: US$3,000-8,000 or US$20,000-40,000. If the surrogate's health plan covers surrogate pregnancy, you pay deductibles and co-pays. If it excludes surrogacy, you need a dedicated policy. Add life and disability cover for the surrogate, and remember the newborn's care before your own insurance applies. Read our insurance guide.

8. Escrow and administration: US$1,500-3,500. An independent escrow company holds funds and pays compensation and bills. This protects both sides; don't skip it.

9. Psychological screening and counselling: US$2,000-5,000.

10. Travel: US$3,000-8,000 domestic, US$8,000-20,000 international, plus citizenship and passport work for parents from abroad.

11. Contingency: plan 10% or more. A failed transfer, twins, a caesarean, bed rest or a rematch can each add thousands.

Costs in altruistic systems

In Canada, surrogates may only be reimbursed for receipted expenses directly related to the pregnancy, such as travel, maternity clothes, medications and, with a doctor's note, lost income. Paying a surrogate, or paying an intermediary to arrange a surrogate's services, is a federal offence. Canadian "consultancies" charge for support services, not for matching. Reimbursements commonly total roughly US$15,000-30,000, plus IVF, legal and counselling costs.

In the UK, a surrogate can receive reasonable expenses; the HFEA notes typical payments of about GBP 10,000-15,000. Anything beyond reasonable expenses must be authorised by the family court before a parental order. Non-profit organisations charge membership or service fees rather than matching fees.

Costs in Mexico, Colombia and Georgia

Programme prices look attractive, but compare the legal risk as carefully as the price. Mexico regulates surrogacy state by state and parentage often needs a court process; Colombia has no statute and a 2026 bill would ban surrogacy; Georgia is open only to married heterosexual couples, and reported waits stretched to as long as three years in 2026. Budget for longer stays, court steps, translations and home-country recognition. Check the law tracker before paying a deposit.

Hidden costs people forget

  • Embryo storage and shipping between clinics or countries.
  • A second or third transfer, often US$5,000-15,000 each.
  • Newborn care before your own insurance applies, including any neonatal intensive care.
  • Second-parent adoption in states or countries that require it.
  • Lost income for your own time off work and travel.
  • Currency movements on international programmes.

How families pay for it

Most families combine several sources: savings, employer fertility benefits (some cover surrogacy-related costs), specialist fertility lenders, personal loans, home-equity borrowing in the US, and help from family. Read every term, compare APRs and avoid borrowing that would leave you without a contingency. Tax treatment of surrogacy costs is limited and varies by country, so ask a tax professional before assuming any deduction or credit.
