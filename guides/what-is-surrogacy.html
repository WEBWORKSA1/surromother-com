Surrogacy is an arrangement in which one person carries and gives birth to a baby for someone else, the intended parent or parents, who will raise the child and become its legal parents. It sounds simple. In practice it brings together medicine, law, money and some of the most personal decisions people ever make, which is why the details matter so much.

The two kinds of surrogacy

Gestational surrogacy is the form used in almost every modern arrangement. An embryo is created through IVF using the eggs and sperm of the intended parents, or of donors, and transferred into the uterus of the surrogate, often called a gestational carrier. She has no genetic connection to the baby.

Traditional surrogacy (also called partial or straight surrogacy) uses the surrogate's own egg, usually through insemination. She is the child's genetic mother. Because that genetic link makes the emotional and legal position far more complicated, most clinics, agencies and lawyers in North America do not support traditional arrangements, and several jurisdictions treat them very differently in law.

Who uses surrogacy, and why

People turn to surrogacy when carrying a pregnancy is impossible or unsafe. Common reasons include:

  • Uterine factors: being born without a uterus, a hysterectomy after cancer or haemorrhage, severe scarring, or significant structural problems.
  • Medical conditions that make pregnancy dangerous, such as serious heart or kidney disease, or a history of severe preeclampsia.
  • Repeated IVF failure or pregnancy loss without an explained cause, after specialist evaluation.
  • Male couples and single men, who need both an egg donor and a gestational carrier.
  • Some transgender and non-binary people building a family.

A fertility specialist should always be part of the decision. Surrogacy is not a shortcut around treatable fertility problems; it is a route for people who genuinely need someone else to carry.

Compensated, altruistic and prohibited

How the surrogate is treated financially is where countries differ most:

  • Compensated (commercial) surrogacy: the surrogate receives payment for her time, effort and risk in addition to expenses. This is lawful in most US states and a handful of other countries, usually with strict eligibility rules.
  • Altruistic surrogacy: the surrogate may only be reimbursed for documented, pregnancy-related expenses. This is the model in Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, India and others. Paying more, or paying a broker to find a surrogate, can be a crime. In Canada, for example, penalties reach C$500,000 and up to ten years in prison.
  • Prohibited: in countries such as France, Germany, Italy, Spain and China, surrogacy agreements are void or banned. Some, like Italy, also penalise citizens who arrange surrogacy abroad.

Who is involved in a surrogacy journey

A typical gestational journey involves a team:

  1. The intended parents, who make the big decisions and pay the costs.
  2. The surrogate and, often, her partner, who must also consent to parts of the process.
  3. A fertility clinic, which creates embryos, screens the surrogate medically and performs the embryo transfer.
  4. Two separate lawyers, one for each side, who negotiate the agreement and handle parentage.
  5. A mental-health professional, who evaluates and counsels the surrogate and often the intended parents.
  6. An agency or programme (optional), which recruits, screens and matches surrogates and coordinates the steps.
  7. An escrow or trust manager, who holds funds and pays compensation or reimbursements.
  8. An obstetrician and hospital, who care for the surrogate once the fertility clinic releases her.

Some people also work with an egg donor programme, an insurance specialist, a financing provider and, for international journeys, an immigration lawyer.

What the journey looks like

Most journeys take 15 to 24 months from the first consultation to bringing a baby home. Matching can be quick in the United States, often one to six months, but in altruistic systems it can take a year or longer because so few surrogates are available. After matching come screening, legal agreements, medication and transfer, nine months of pregnancy, and then the legal steps that make the intended parents the child's parents.

The ethical debate, briefly

Surrogacy divides opinion. Supporters see it as a way to build families and as an expression of the surrogate's own choice, provided she is well informed, fairly treated and legally protected. Critics worry about exploitation, especially across borders and where women have few economic options, and about the commodification of pregnancy. In 2025 and 2026 several countries tightened their rules, while others, such as Thailand and Ireland, worked on new frameworks. Wherever you stand, the practical safeguards are the same: independent legal advice for the surrogate, transparent payments through escrow, proper medical and psychological screening, and respect for the surrogate's right to make decisions about her own body.
