Insurance is where many surrogacy budgets go wrong. A surprise exclusion can add tens of thousands of dollars, and a gap in newborn cover can be even more expensive if the baby needs intensive care. Here's how to get it right. Insurance rules are country- and plan-specific, so take specialist advice.

The four kinds of cover in a journey

  1. The surrogate's health coverage for the pregnancy and delivery.
  2. Life (and often disability) insurance for the surrogate, so her family is protected.
  3. Coverage for IVF and embryo creation (intended parents or egg provider).
  4. The newborn's coverage from birth.

1. The surrogate's health plan (US)

In the US, the key question is whether the surrogate's own plan covers surrogate pregnancies. Many do; some contain surrogacy exclusions, and some include clauses that let the insurer seek reimbursement from the surrogate's compensation. An insurance specialist should read the full plan document, not just the summary, before matching or at least before medications start.

  • If the plan covers surrogacy: intended parents typically pay deductibles, co-pays and out-of-pocket maximums, often a few thousand dollars.
  • If it excludes surrogacy: intended parents buy a dedicated surrogacy maternity policy, which commonly costs US$20,000-40,000. Check what it excludes, such as complications or pre-existing conditions.
  • Never ask a surrogate to hide the nature of the pregnancy from her insurer.

2. Life and disability insurance

Most agreements require a term life policy for the surrogate for the length of the journey and some months after, paid for by the intended parents, and many add disability or lost-income cover. In Canada and other expenses-only systems, life insurance premiums are a typical reimbursable cost.

3. IVF and embryo creation

Some employer benefits cover IVF for the intended parents, and a few cover parts of a surrogacy journey. Check benefit documents and ask HR directly. Donor-egg cycles and genetic testing are often excluded.

4. The newborn

The baby is not normally covered by the surrogate's plan. Plan to:

  • add the baby to the intended parents' health insurance immediately (many plans require notification within 30 days);
  • confirm whether your plan covers a baby born via surrogacy before you have a parentage order;
  • for international parents, buy newborn cover for the time in the birth country, including neonatal intensive care, which can cost thousands of dollars a day.

Outside the US

In Canada, provincial health insurance generally covers the surrogate's medical care as it would any pregnancy; intended parents usually cover life insurance and other receipted expenses. In the UK, NHS maternity care is available to surrogates as residents. In Mexico, Colombia and Georgia, programmes often include a medical reserve, but read what happens with complications, caesareans or neonatal care, and budget extra.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Who reviewed the surrogate's plan, and do we have that review in writing?
  • If a separate policy is needed, what is excluded and what are the limits?
  • Who pays deductibles, co-pays and uncovered bills, and from which account?
  • What life and disability cover will the surrogate have, and for how long?
  • How and when will the baby be covered?
