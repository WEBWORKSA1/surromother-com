An agency can make a surrogacy journey smoother, safer and faster, or, if you choose badly, slower, more expensive and painful for everyone. Because most countries regulate agencies lightly or not at all, the job of checking falls on you. This guide explains what a good agency does, the questions that separate strong programmes from weak ones, and the red flags to walk away from.

What a surrogacy agency actually does

A full-service agency typically:

  • recruits prospective surrogates and does an initial records review;
  • screens them medically, psychologically and with background checks (sometimes before matching, sometimes after);
  • matches surrogates and intended parents on values, communication and logistics;
  • coordinates the clinic, lawyers, insurance, escrow and travel;
  • supports both sides through the pregnancy and after the birth.

What an agency should not do: hold your money directly, tell the surrogate which medical decisions to make, or pressure either side to sign quickly.

In Canada, a business cannot lawfully charge to arrange a surrogate's services, so Canadian providers offer consultation and support services instead. In the UK, commercial surrogacy agencies are prohibited and non-profit organisations fill the gap. Make sure any provider you use is operating lawfully where it works.

Six things to check, in order

1. Licensing and track record

Ask which licences or registrations the agency holds (New York, for example, licenses surrogacy programmes) and look them up. Ask how long it has operated, how many journeys it completed last year, and whether it belongs to professional or ethics organisations. Search for complaints, court cases and news coverage.

2. How surrogates are screened and supported

Good agencies screen surrogates against recognised medical guidance, use licensed mental-health professionals, run background checks on surrogates and their partners, and give surrogates their own independent lawyer paid for by the intended parents. Ask what support surrogates receive during and after the pregnancy. Agencies that treat surrogates well have surrogates who come back for second journeys and refer friends.

3. Matching

Ask for the current average match time for someone in your situation, how many screened surrogates are available now, and whether they work with single parents, same-sex couples and international families. Ask how they handle differences of view on prenatal testing, reduction or termination before a match is confirmed.

4. Money: fees, refunds and escrow

Ask for a written, itemised fee schedule that lists every possible cost, including rematch fees and contingencies. Find out which fees are refundable and when. Insist that funds go to an independent, licensed escrow company that sends regular statements. Ask what happens to your money if the agency closes.

5. Insurance and medical partners

Who reviews the surrogate's health insurance for surrogacy exclusions? If a separate policy is needed, who arranges it and what does it exclude? Which IVF clinics does the agency work with, and what are their success rates with gestational carriers specifically?

6. Legal and after the birth

Which state's law will govern the agreement? Will you get a pre-birth order? Who handles the birth certificate and, for international parents, citizenship and passports? What is the plan if there is a dispute?

Red flags

Walk away, or at least slow down, if you see any of these:

  • No written fee schedule, or fees that change after you sign.
  • The agency holds your funds in its own account.
  • Large upfront payments before a surrogate is identified or screened.
  • Guarantees of a baby or of "unlimited" transfers at a low fixed price, without clear terms.
  • Very fast match promises with no explanation of how surrogates are screened.
  • Reluctance to let you speak to recent intended parents and surrogates.
  • Pressure tactics, such as discounts that expire tomorrow.
  • Programmes in countries where surrogacy is banned or restricted for foreigners, or that ask you to move embryos or surrogates across borders to get around the law.

Agency failures do happen. In December 2025 a US agency closed abruptly, leaving pregnant surrogates without payments and parents without answers; NBC News reported an FBI investigation. Independent escrow and clear contracts are what protect you if a business fails.

Comparing quotes fairly

Agencies present prices differently: some quote only their own fee, others an "all-inclusive" estimate. To compare like for like, map every quote against the same line items: agency fee, surrogate compensation and allowances, medical, legal, insurance, escrow, counselling, travel and contingencies. Our cost calculator uses exactly these lines.

When an agency isn't the right choice

If you already know your surrogate, such as a friend or relative, an independent journey with a specialist lawyer, clinic and escrow company may suit you better and cost less.
