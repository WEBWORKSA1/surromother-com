Money is the part of surrogacy that the law treats most differently around the world. Before thinking about amounts, every surrogate and intended parent needs to know which system applies to them: compensated, expenses-only (altruistic), or not permitted. This guide explains each, so you can understand what is lawful, what is fair and what to watch for.

SurroMother does not pay, recruit or arrange surrogates. This page is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Which system applies to you?

The rules that matter are usually those of the place where the surrogate lives and gives birth, together with the intended parents' home country. Check the law tracker.

System Examples What a surrogate may receive
Compensated Most US states Base compensation plus expenses under a written agreement
Expenses only Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand, India, South Africa Documented, pregnancy-related costs only
Not permitted France, Germany, Italy, Spain, China and others Surrogacy agreements are void or banned

Where compensation is lawful (most US states)

In US states that permit it, a surrogacy agreement sets out a compensation package. Agencies publish base compensation for first-time surrogates of roughly US$50,000-80,000 in 2026, higher for experienced surrogates and in premium states, though amounts vary by agency and state. A package typically includes:

  • Base compensation, usually paid monthly once a heartbeat is confirmed.
  • A monthly allowance for incidental costs such as local travel and phone calls.
  • Event fees: medication start, each embryo transfer, invasive procedures, a caesarean delivery, or carrying multiples.
  • Reimbursements: lost wages with documentation, childcare and housekeeping during bed rest, maternity clothes, travel and lodging for appointments.
  • Insurance: health coverage for the pregnancy, plus life and often disability insurance.
  • Post-birth support, such as recovery time and counselling.

A fair package is transparent, paid through an independent escrow account, and never conditioned on a particular medical decision. The surrogate's own lawyer, paid for by the intended parents, reviews it before anything is signed.

Taxes. In the US, many tax professionals treat surrogate compensation as taxable income, while documented reimbursements are treated differently. Practice varies, so ask a tax professional.

Where only expenses are allowed

Canada

Under the Assisted Human Reproduction Act, it is a federal offence to pay a surrogate, offer to pay her, or advertise that payment will be made, and to pay or accept payment for arranging a surrogate's services. Penalties reach C$500,000 and/or ten years in prison. A surrogate may be reimbursed for receipted expenditures directly related to the pregnancy, under 2020 regulations, which can include travel, medication, maternity clothing, counselling, legal advice and, with a medical certificate, lost work income. Keep every receipt.

United Kingdom

A surrogate may receive reasonable expenses. The UK fertility regulator notes typical payments of around GBP 10,000-15,000. Agreements are not legally enforceable, commercial agencies are banned, and the family court checks payments before granting the parental order.

Australia and New Zealand

Both allow altruistic surrogacy with reimbursement of reasonable expenses such as medical, legal, counselling and travel costs and some lost earnings. Commercial surrogacy is a criminal offence in Australia, and residents of New South Wales, Queensland and the ACT also commit an offence by arranging it overseas.

India

Since the Surrogacy (Regulation) Act 2021, only altruistic surrogacy for eligible Indian couples is allowed. The surrogate may receive medical expenses and the intended couple must provide insurance cover for her. Commercial surrogacy, and advertising it in any form, are criminal offences.

Where surrogacy is not permitted

In countries that prohibit surrogacy, agreements are void and there is no lawful payment. Please don't travel to another country to act as a surrogate without independent legal advice: investigations in several countries have found women recruited across borders who were underpaid, isolated or mistreated.

Protecting yourself, whatever the system

  • Your own lawyer, independent from the intended parents' lawyer.
  • Escrow or trust account with statements you can see.
  • Everything in writing, including what happens if a transfer fails, the pregnancy ends early, or there are complications.
  • No upfront fees. Reputable programmes never ask surrogates to pay to apply or register.
  • Insurance reviewed before medications start.
  • Medical autonomy: payment must never depend on agreeing to a particular medical decision.
