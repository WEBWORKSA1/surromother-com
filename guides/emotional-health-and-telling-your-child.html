Surrogacy is a medical and legal process, but people describe it first as an emotional one. Intended parents often arrive after years of infertility or loss; surrogates take on a pregnancy for someone else and then go home without the baby. Paying attention to wellbeing is not a soft extra. It's what keeps journeys steady when something goes wrong.

For intended parents

Grief is allowed. Many intended mothers and parents grieve the pregnancy they won't carry, even while feeling hopeful. Counselling before you start helps you separate that grief from your relationship with the surrogate.

Uncertainty is the hardest part. Waiting for a match, a transfer result or a scan can be exhausting. Agree how and when you'll receive news so you're not constantly refreshing your phone.

Control is limited. You are paying for much of the journey, but the surrogate makes decisions about her own body. Families who accept this early find the pregnancy far less stressful.

For surrogates

Your family is part of the journey. Partners take on extra childcare and support; your own children will notice the pregnancy and need an explanation suited to their age, such as "we're helping another family grow their baby".

Mixed feelings are normal. Pride, tiredness, attachment to the intended parents, worry about complications and, after the birth, a sense of loss or anticlimax. Counselling during and after the pregnancy should be part of any good arrangement.

You are a partner, not a service. Respectful communication, prompt payments or reimbursements, and recognition of your effort are reasonable expectations.

The relationship between you

Good surrogacy relationships share a few habits:

  • Agreed communication: how often, which channels, and who else is included (partners, grandparents).
  • Clear boundaries: attending appointments, social media, and what each person wants after the birth.
  • Honesty about difficult topics, discussed with a counsellor before matching: testing, reduction and termination.
  • Kindness when plans change: a failed transfer, a complication, or a different birth than hoped for.

If tensions build, ask your agency, programme or counsellor for a facilitated conversation early, before they harden.

When things go wrong

Failed transfers, miscarriage and complications happen. They are hard for everyone, and they are no one's fault. Pause, get support, and talk about next steps only when you're ready. Many agreements allow several transfers; your clinic can explain the medical picture.

Telling your child their story

Research on families created through donor conception and surrogacy consistently supports openness from an early age. Children cope best when their origin story is something they have always known rather than a revelation later.

  • Start early, before age four if you can, with simple, positive language: "A kind woman called Sarah carried you in her tummy because our bodies couldn't."
  • Use books and photos from the journey; many families keep a scrapbook.
  • Answer questions honestly and at the child's level, and expect the same questions again as they grow.
  • Include donors where relevant: "Some of the cells that made you came from a donor who wanted to help."
  • Think about contact: if the surrogate and family agree, some children grow up knowing her as a special person in their lives.

Support

Counselling with a professional experienced in third-party reproduction is the most valuable support. Peer communities, such as those run by non-profits in the US, UK and Australia, connect you with people who have been there. If you feel persistently low, anxious or unable to cope, speak to your doctor.
