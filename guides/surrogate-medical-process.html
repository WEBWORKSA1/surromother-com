Becoming a gestational surrogate is, medically, a pregnancy created through IVF. You don't go through egg stimulation or retrieval (those happen to the egg provider), but you do take medications to prepare your uterus, have a short transfer procedure, and receive closer monitoring early on than in a typical pregnancy. Here is what to expect, stage by stage. Your clinic's protocol may differ, so always follow your own doctors' instructions.

1. Screening (2-6 weeks)

At the IVF clinic you'll have:

  • a physical exam and review of your previous pregnancy and delivery records;
  • blood tests, including hormone levels, blood type and infectious-disease screening (often for your partner too);
  • a check of the inside of your uterus, such as a saline sonogram or hysteroscopy;
  • sometimes a cervical screening test and other routine checks.

You'll also meet a mental-health professional. Screening is designed to protect you; ask anything.

2. Getting ready: the medication calendar (3-6 weeks)

Once the legal agreement is signed, the clinic gives you a calendar. A typical frozen-embryo protocol includes:

  • Birth control pills for a few weeks to time your cycle.
  • Sometimes a suppression medication to quiet your natural hormones.
  • Estrogen (pills, patches or injections) for about two weeks to thicken the uterine lining.
  • Progesterone (injections, vaginal tablets or gels) starting a few days before transfer, to make the lining receptive.

You'll have monitoring appointments for blood tests and ultrasounds, sometimes at a local clinic near home. If your lining isn't ready, the clinic may adjust doses or postpone. Some clinics do a mock cycle first.

3. Embryo transfer (one morning)

The transfer itself takes a few minutes. Using ultrasound guidance, the doctor passes a thin catheter through the cervix and places the embryo in the uterus. Most surrogates go home or back to their hotel the same day and rest for a short time, following clinic advice. The intended parents may attend.

4. The two-week wait (9-12 days)

You continue medications and wait. A pregnancy blood test (beta hCG) usually happens about nine to ten days after a day-5 embryo transfer, often repeated two days later to see the level rising. Our due date calculator shows this date and every milestone after.

If the test is negative, you stop medications and have a period. Many agreements allow two or three transfers; the next attempt can usually start after a cycle or two.

5. Early pregnancy at the fertility clinic (to about 8-10 weeks)

You keep taking estrogen and progesterone, often until about 10-12 weeks, while the placenta takes over hormone production. Ultrasounds confirm the pregnancy is in the uterus and, at about six to seven weeks, show a heartbeat. In many US arrangements, base compensation begins once a heartbeat is confirmed.

Around 8-10 weeks, the clinic releases you to your obstetrician or midwife.

6. Pregnancy care (to delivery)

From here, care is much like any pregnancy: regular appointments, screening tests (such as non-invasive prenatal testing from about 10 weeks, the anatomy scan at about 20 weeks, and glucose screening at 24-28 weeks), and growth checks. Some intended parents ask for extra monitoring, which should be agreed in the contract.

Agree in advance how appointment updates are shared, and remember that you make decisions about your own medical care, with your doctor.

7. Delivery

Your birth plan should cover who is in the room, whether the intended parents hold the baby first, feeding plans, and hospital paperwork. Many hospitals have a surrogacy process. Your lawyer and the intended parents' lawyer will make sure the hospital has the parentage order or knows the legal process that applies.

8. Recovery and afterwards

Physical recovery is like any birth: usually around six weeks, longer after a caesarean. Hormonal changes after birth can affect mood; some surrogates choose to pump breast milk for the baby, which should be agreed and supported. Counselling should be available after the birth, not just before. Many surrogates describe a mix of pride, relief and missing the closeness of the journey.

Risks to discuss with your doctor

  • Side effects from medications (injection-site soreness, bloating, mood changes).
  • Pregnancy complications such as high blood pressure, preeclampsia and gestational diabetes.
  • Twins, if more than one embryo is transferred or an embryo splits.
  • Caesarean delivery and, rarely, serious bleeding or hysterectomy.

Screening, single-embryo transfer and good prenatal care reduce these risks but cannot remove them.
