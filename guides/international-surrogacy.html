When surrogacy is banned, unavailable or very slow at home, many families look abroad. International surrogacy can work well, but it adds a layer that domestic journeys don't have: your child must be recognised as yours, and as a citizen, by your own country. A birth certificate from the birth country is not enough on its own.

The three-country test

Before choosing a destination, check the law in three places:

  1. Where you live and hold citizenship. Does your country recognise foreign surrogacy, and how? Does it penalise its citizens for using surrogacy abroad?
  2. Where the surrogate lives. Is surrogacy lawful there, for people like you, and on what payment terms?
  3. Where the baby will be born. Who is named on the birth certificate, and how is parentage confirmed?

Citizenship and passports

Rules differ widely:

  • Some countries pass citizenship only through a genetic link to a citizen parent and require DNA testing.
  • Some require a domestic court order or adoption to recognise a non-genetic parent, as Spain has since its April 2025 instruction and as the UK requires through a parental order even when a foreign birth certificate names the intended parents.
  • A few criminalise their citizens for arranging surrogacy abroad. Italy made surrogacy prosecutable abroad in 2024.

Expect weeks of paperwork after the birth, and budget for longer stays. The UK fertility regulator warns that getting a passport and bringing a child home after an overseas surrogacy can be slow and difficult.

The United States after August 2026

The US remains the most legally developed destination, with pre-birth orders in many states. But international parents face new uncertainty:

  • On 30 June 2026, the Supreme Court upheld birthright citizenship in Trump v. Barbara.
  • On 6 August 2026, executive orders sought to deny federal recognition of citizenship in certain circumstances, including some commercial surrogacy arrangements where neither parent is a US citizen, and to restrict visas for "birth tourism".
  • The orders were challenged in court and agency guidance was pending at our last review.
  • Separately, Florida's FIRE Act (from 1 July 2026) bars parties who are citizens or residents of seven designated countries from Florida surrogacy agreements.

Even if your child does not need US citizenship to travel home, the practical steps of getting documents and leaving the US could be affected. Get advice from a US immigration lawyer and from your own country's consulate before transfer.

Destinations that changed in 2025-2026

  • Greece: since May 2025, the intended mother and the surrogate must be legal residents; single men and male couples are excluded.
  • Spain: since April 2025, foreign birth certificates and court orders are not accepted to register surrogacy births; in February 2026 prosecutors also targeted agency websites advertising to Spanish residents.
  • Kazakhstan: an amendment signed in July 2026 limits surrogacy to legally married Kazakh citizens.
  • Georgia: still open to married heterosexual couples, but reported waits grew to as long as three years in 2026 and a ban for foreigners has been proposed before.
  • Ukraine: legal for married heterosexual couples, but the war, a major agency's bankruptcy in early 2026 and consular delays make it very high risk.
  • Colombia: no statute; a bill filed in August 2026 would ban surrogacy.
  • Thailand: a draft law in consultation would admit some foreign married couples on an altruistic basis; commercial surrogacy stays banned.

How to reduce risk

  • Get home-country advice first, in writing, from a family and immigration lawyer.
  • Prefer jurisdictions with statutes, not just court practice.
  • Use independent escrow and keep originals of every document.
  • Plan for time: post-birth paperwork can take one to three months.
  • Have a contingency fund for longer stays, legal steps and medical costs for the baby.
  • Avoid programmes that move surrogates across borders to sidestep local law. Investigations in several countries have found exploitation in these arrangements.
  • Think about your child's future access to their story and, where possible, to the surrogate and any donors.
