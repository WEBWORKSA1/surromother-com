Requirements for surrogates exist for one main reason: to keep the surrogate and the baby as safe as possible. They are set by clinics and programmes, informed by professional guidance such as the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's recommendations for gestational carriers, and sometimes by law. Exact limits vary, so treat the figures below as typical, not universal.

The usual requirements, and why

Age: about 21 to 40 (sometimes up to 45)

Most programmes accept surrogates from 21. In Canada, advising or performing procedures to help someone under 21 become a surrogate is prohibited by law. Upper limits are usually 40 to 45, because pregnancy risks rise with age.

At least one previous healthy pregnancy and delivery

Clinics want evidence that you have carried a pregnancy to term without serious complications. Many programmes also prefer that you are raising a child of your own, partly because it means you understand what pregnancy and parenting involve.

A healthy BMI

Most clinics look for a body mass index of roughly 19-32; some accept up to 35. Higher BMI increases the risk of preeclampsia, gestational diabetes and caesarean delivery. Our pre-screen calculates your BMI for you.

No nicotine, vaping or drug use

A smoke-free and drug-free household is standard. Most programmes ask for at least a year without nicotine.

No serious complications in past pregnancies

Conditions such as preeclampsia, placenta problems, severe bleeding after birth or preterm birth usually need a doctor's review and are often exclusions. Gestational diabetes may be accepted after review.

Limits on deliveries and caesareans

Many clinics accept no more than five previous deliveries and two or three caesareans, because each surgery and pregnancy adds risk.

Time since your last birth

Clinics usually want 6-12 months after a vaginal delivery and often 12 months after a caesarean before starting medications, and you must have stopped breastfeeding.

Living where surrogacy is lawful

Surrogacy law depends on where you live and where the baby will be born. In some places surrogates can be compensated, in many only reimbursed for expenses, and in some surrogacy is not allowed. Check the law tracker for your country or the US state guide.

Stable life and support

Programmes look for a stable home, a support person, and the ability to attend appointments. Many require that you are not relying on certain forms of public assistance, to make sure no one is pressured into surrogacy by financial hardship.

Screening: what to expect

If you pass the initial checks, you'll go through:

  • A records review of your previous pregnancies and deliveries.
  • A medical evaluation at the IVF clinic: physical exam, blood tests, infectious-disease screening, and an evaluation of your uterus (such as a saline sonogram or hysteroscopy).
  • A psychological evaluation with a licensed mental-health professional, often including standard questionnaires and an interview with you and your partner.
  • Background checks for you and your partner.

Screening protects you as much as the intended parents. It's also a chance to ask your own questions.

Questions to ask yourself first

  • Does my partner support this? Do my children understand, in a way that fits their age?
  • Can I attend frequent appointments in the first trimester?
  • How will I feel about taking medications and injections?
  • Am I comfortable with the intended parents' views on testing and termination?
  • What support will I have during recovery?
