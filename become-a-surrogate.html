For prospective surrogates

Thinking about carrying a baby for another family?

It is one of the most generous things a person can do, and a serious medical, legal and emotional commitment. Here is what it involves, without the sales pitch.

Step one: is it lawful where you live?

Surrogacy law depends on where you live, not where the parents live. In some places surrogates can be compensated; in many, only documented expenses can be reimbursed; in others it is not permitted at all.

What clinics usually look for

  • Aged about 21-40 (some clinics up to 45)
  • At least one healthy, full-term pregnancy and delivery
  • Raising a child of your own (preferred by most programmes)
  • BMI usually between 19 and 32 (some accept up to 35)
  • No nicotine, vaping or drug use
  • No serious complications such as preeclampsia in past pregnancies
  • Usually no more than five deliveries or three caesareans
  • A stable home and a support person
  • Willing to complete medical, psychological and background screening

Every clinic sets its own criteria, informed by professional guidance such as the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's recommendations for gestational carriers.

Your rights

What a fair arrangement looks like

  • Your own lawyer, chosen by you and paid for by the intended parents.
  • Your medical decisions remain yours.
  • Health insurance reviewed before transfer, plus life insurance.
  • All payments or reimbursements held by an independent escrow or trust account.
  • Counselling before, during and after the pregnancy.
  • A clear plan for the birth and the weeks afterwards.

The journey, in order

  1. Learn and talk it through at home

    Partner, children, employer and support network. The commitment usually spans 12-18 months.

  2. Application and records review

    Your pregnancy and delivery records are reviewed by the clinic or programme.

  3. Matching

    You meet intended parents and agree on communication, values and expectations.

  4. Screening

    Clinic exam, blood tests, uterine evaluation, psychological assessment and background checks.

  5. Legal agreement

    Negotiated by your own lawyer before any medication begins.

  6. Medication and transfer

    Usually estrogen and progesterone for several weeks, then a short, usually painless transfer.

  7. Pregnancy and birth

    Early monitoring at the fertility clinic, then your chosen obstetrician.

  8. Recovery and after

    Postpartum care, counselling and the relationship you agreed with the family.

Payment: what the law allows

Where compensation is lawful

In most US states, surrogates receive base compensation plus expenses under a written agreement, paid through escrow. Amounts vary by state and experience; our compensation guide explains how packages are built.

Where only expenses are allowed

In Canada, the UK, Australia, India and many other countries, surrogates may only be reimbursed for documented pregnancy-related costs. Payment beyond that, or paying someone to arrange it, can be a criminal offence.

Where it is not permitted

In countries such as France, Germany, Italy, Spain and China, surrogacy agreements are void or prohibited. Please don't travel abroad to act as a surrogate without independent legal advice.

Honest risks to weigh

Medical

All the risks of pregnancy and birth, including high blood pressure, gestational diabetes, caesarean delivery and, rarely, hysterectomy, plus side effects of IVF medications. Single-embryo transfer reduces the chance of twins.

Emotional and practical

Time away from work and family, relationship strain, feelings after the birth, and the possibility that the relationship with the family changes. Counselling and a good support network help.

Legal and financial

Unclear laws, unenforceable agreements in some places, and agencies that fail. Independent lawyers and escrow are your protection.

Scams

Be wary of anyone asking you to travel abroad, hand over documents, or pay a fee to "register". Reputable programmes never charge surrogates.
